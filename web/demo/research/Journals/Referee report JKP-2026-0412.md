# Referee report JKP-2026-0412

Manuscript: "Second brains: personal knowledge management and the myth of the extended mind". Journal of Knowledge Practices. Recommendation: major revision. Sent 2026-11-06.

## Summary of the submission

The manuscript examines the claims made for personal knowledge management software and argues that they rest on a loose reading of the extended mind thesis. It combines a conceptual argument with a small survey of users.

## Assessment

The question is timely and the conceptual section is the strongest part. The author is right that popular writing treats any digital notebook as part of the mind, while the original argument sets conditions on reliability, access and endorsement [@clark1998]. The survey, however, cannot carry the weight placed on it: it asks users whether their notes feel like part of their thinking, which measures belief, not coupling.

## Main points

1. The paper would gain from distributed cognition, which offers a better unit of analysis for shared tools than the individual mind [@hutchins1995].
2. The historical claims are thin. Note-taking techniques for managing abundance have a long history [@blair2010; @krajewski2011], and Luhmann's index is presented without the context the archival work provides [@schmidt2016].
3. The section on learning cites the laptop note-taking study as settled [@mueller2014]; later replication attempts have been mixed, and the claim should be softened. Work on retrieval practice is more robust and more relevant to the argument [@roediger2006].
4. The conclusion moves from "notes are not minds" to "notes do not matter", which does not follow.

## Minor points

- The abstract promises a typology that never arrives.
- Several references are to blog posts where a primary source exists (for example [@luhmann1981]).

## Confidential comments to the editor

A promising paper by someone who knows the software well. Worth a second round if the survey is reframed as exploratory.
