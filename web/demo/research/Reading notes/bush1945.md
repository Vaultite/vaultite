## Summary

Written at the end of the war by the director of the US Office of Scientific Research and Development, the essay asks what scientists should turn to once weapons research winds down, and answers: making the growing record of knowledge usable. Its centrepiece is the memex, a desk-sized device that stores a person's books, records and correspondence on microfilm and lets them tie items together into trails they can follow and share.

## Notes

- The diagnosis is about retrieval, not storage: the record is growing faster than anyone can consult it, and indexing by class and subclass is too rigid.
- The alternative is association: Bush contrasts the hierarchical filing systems of libraries with the way the mind jumps from one item to the next.
- Trails are the key idea. A trail is a named sequence of linked items that persists and can be handed to a colleague, which makes it a published object, not just a private habit.
- He imagines a new profession of trail blazers who build useful paths through the record for others.
- Almost all of the technology is wrong (microfilm, dry photography), and almost none of the problem has changed.

## Connections

- The trail anticipates Nelson's links [[Draft]] and Luhmann's numbered sequences of cards [[luhmann1981]].
