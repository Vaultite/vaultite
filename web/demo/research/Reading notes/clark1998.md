## Summary

The paper argues for active externalism: when parts of the environment are coupled to a cognitive process in the right way, they are part of that process. The famous case is Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease and relies on a notebook, set beside Inga, who relies on biological memory to find a museum. If the notebook plays the same role as memory, Clark and Chalmers argue, it should count as memory.

## Notes

- Opens with the question that frames the whole debate: "Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin?" (p. 7).
- Parity principle: if a process in the world would count as cognitive were it done in the head, it is cognitive.
- Conditions for the notebook: reliably available, information endorsed more or less automatically, easily accessed, and endorsed in the past.
- The examples are deliberately mundane (Tetris, a notebook), which is what makes them useful for thinking about note systems.
- Critics ask whether coupling is enough to make something cognitive; the paper concedes the conditions are matters of degree.
