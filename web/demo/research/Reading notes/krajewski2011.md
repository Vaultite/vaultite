## Summary

A media history of the card index from Konrad Gessner's cut-up excerpts in the sixteenth century to the card files of the early twentieth-century office. Krajewski reads the index as a paper machine, a device that performs operations of storage, addressing and retrieval, and argues that it anticipates the logic of the computer.

## Notes

- Starting point: Gessner's practice of writing excerpts on sheets and cutting them up so they could be rearranged.
- Library catalogues move from bound volumes to cards in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, which separates the catalogue from any single order.
- Around 1900 the card index leaves the library for the office and becomes a product, sold with systems and advice.
- The comparison with the universal discrete machine is suggestive but sometimes pressed too hard.
- Translation is readable; some German wordplay is lost.
