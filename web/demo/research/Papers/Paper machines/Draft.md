# Introduction

Scholars have always needed somewhere to put what they cannot keep in their heads. When Vannevar Bush imagined the memex, a desk that would hold a researcher's books, records and correspondence and let them be followed by association rather than by index [@bush1945], he was answering a problem that early modern compilers already knew well: too much to read and too little memory to hold it [@blair2010]. Between those two moments lies a long history of slips, drawers and catalogues that historians now treat as machines in their own right [@krajewski2011]. This paper argues that such note systems are best understood not as storage but as infrastructure: largely invisible arrangements that shape what a researcher can notice, connect and eventually write [@star1999; @bowker1999].

None of the parts of this claim is new. Philosophers of mind have argued that a notebook can be part of a cognitive process rather than a mere aid to it [@clark1998; @hutchins1995], and sociologists of science have shown how inscriptions move through a laboratory until they harden into facts [see @latour1986, pp. 45-48]. What is missing is an account that joins these literatures to the everyday practice of keeping notes: the card that is written, filed, linked and found again years later. Niklas Luhmann's description of his own card index as a partner in communication [@luhmann1981, p. 225] offers a starting point, and it has recently travelled well beyond sociology [@ahrens2017; @schmidt2016].

The argument proceeds in three steps. The next section places the card index in a longer history of paper technologies. The third reads Luhmann's account of his index against theories of extended and distributed cognition. The fourth follows the link, the smallest unit of a note system, from the card to the citation index, and asks what it means that the same structures organise both private notes and the public literature.

# Notes before computers

The problem of information overload is older than the printed book, but print sharpened it. @blair2010 shows how sixteenth-century scholars developed a repertoire of techniques for managing abundance: commonplace books with headings, florilegia, alphabetical indexes and, decisively, notes taken on loose slips that could be cut, sorted and pasted into new orders. Konrad Gessner's practice of cutting up excerpts to rearrange them is, for @krajewski2011, the first moment of a technology that runs through library catalogues to the card files of the early twentieth-century office.[^1]

What these techniques share is a separation between the moment of reading and the moment of writing. A slip is written once and can be placed many times. Paul Otlet made this principle explicit when he proposed that knowledge be broken into its smallest units, each on its own card, so that it could be classified and recombined independently of the book it came from [@otlet1934]. The ambition was universal, and the Mundaneum never reached it, but the principle outlived the institution.

Anthropologists of writing would not be surprised. @goody1977 argued that the list and the table are not neutral containers but tools that make certain operations of thought possible, and @ong1982 that writing restructures the very habits of mind it records. A card index is a list that can be rearranged: it extends these effects from the page to the drawer.

# Luhmann's partner

Luhmann's short essay is unusual in that it describes a working system from the inside. Its central technical decisions are simple. Each card receives a fixed number that never changes; a new card is placed after the one it continues, so that lines of thought branch rather than queue; cards point to other cards by number; and a sparse keyword register gives a few entry points into the whole [@luhmann1981]. None of these is new on its own. Together, Luhmann suggests, they give the collection enough internal complexity to surprise the person who wrote it, which is the condition for treating it as a partner rather than an archive [@luhmann1981, p. 225].

The archival work on the index, which ran to roughly ninety thousand cards by the end of Luhmann's life, confirms that the essay understates its messiness and its dependence on routine [@schmidt2016]. The popular literature has, understandably, kept the method and dropped the context. @ahrens2017 argues that the slip box works because it forces the writer to restate each idea in their own words and to decide where it connects, which is a claim about cognition as much as about filing.

# The extended note

That claim can be made precise. On the parity principle of @clark1998, a notebook that is reliably available, automatically endorsed and easily consulted plays the same functional role as biological memory. @hutchins1995 goes further: in his study of ship navigation, the cognitive work is done by a system of people, charts and instruments, and no single participant holds the whole computation. A card index is a small instance of the same arrangement, with one human and many slips. Much of what it holds is the kind of knowledge that @polanyi1966 called tacit, carried less in what the cards say than in where they sit.

Experimental psychology offers a caution. Note-taking that merely transcribes does little for understanding [@mueller2014], and what helps memory is retrieval rather than storage [@roediger2006]. A system that removes all effort may also remove the processing that made notes useful; a system that adds too much effort competes with the task itself [@sweller1988].

# From links to citations

The link is where private notes and public knowledge meet. Douglas Engelbart framed computing as an augmentation of the whole system of human, language, artefacts and method [@engelbart1962], and Ted Nelson's proposal for a file structure built of linkable, revisable entries introduced the word hypertext [@nelson1965]. A decade earlier, Eugene Garfield had argued that a citation index could organise the scientific literature by association of ideas rather than by subject heading [@garfield1955], and @price1965 drew the resulting network to show how a research front forms.

These networks are not neutral either. @merton1968 described how recognition accumulates on those who already have it, and a well-linked card, like a well-cited paper, is the one most likely to be found again. Kuhn's [-@kuhn1962] account of normal science can be read in the same register: a field's shared exemplars are the entry points of its collective index.[^2] Latour's centres of calculation, where inscriptions gathered from elsewhere are combined and compared [@latour1987, ch. 6], describe at the scale of an institution what a card index does on a desk. Where a note system is shared, it becomes a boundary object, robust enough to hold a common identity and plastic enough to serve different users [@star1989].

# Conclusion

Note systems are infrastructure in Star's sense: they sink into practice, become visible mainly when they fail, and carry the conventions of the communities that use them [@star1999]. Studying them as such means asking not what a researcher stored but what their system made findable.
